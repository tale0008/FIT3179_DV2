Australia reads comics. Australia doesn't make them.

Who makes the world's comics, and who only reads them, 1930 to 2026

A comic can be made in Australia, or made somewhere else and printed here. This page follows both: comics made in Australia, comics made in Japan and the United States, and the reprints that carry them around the world.

Unless a chart says otherwise, these figures count comic issues, not copies sold. They come from the Grand Comics Database, a volunteer catalogue of over two million comic issues from around the world.

Or click a country in any chart that compares countries.

By Thisum Patabadige · October 2026 · Sources listed at the end of the page

§1Comics are everywhere. They are not made everywhere.

Almost every country reads comics. Far fewer make them, and the ones that do have made them at different times, in different amounts, and about different things.

Two countries make most of the comics Australians know. The United States gave the world the superhero: DC published Superman in 1938 and Batman a year later, and Marvel followed in the 1960s with Spider-Man and the X-Men. Japan makes manga, the comics behind Dragon Ball, One Piece and Naruto, and it is now the biggest comics market in the world.

Start with where comics come from. Counted in total, a handful of large countries publish most of the world's comics.

Counted per person, the map changes. Small countries in northern Europe turn the darkest.

Norway has about 10,600 comic issues for every million people, around six times the rate of the United States. Australia sits at about 1,300 per million.

(The database is built by volunteers, so this map also measures how thoroughly each country's comics have been catalogued. Japan, the largest comics market in the world, shows only about 1,100 per million for that reason.)

So who makes comics depends on how you count. The next question is when.

Comic industries did not grow at the same time. Some countries had their boom before television arrived, others are still in theirs.

Australia's strip has two humps. When the Second World War began, the government banned American comics, and local artists filled the newsstands with heroes of their own, like the Crimson Comet and Captain Atom. That boom peaked in the mid-1950s. Then comics were censored from 1954, television arrived in 1956 and American comics came back in 1959, and most local publishers closed.

The second hump, around 1980, was mostly American comics printed here, such as Superman and Batman. From the mid-1980s, as shops began importing American comics directly, the strip almost disappears.

The United States had a small hump in the early 1950s, cut short in 1954 when a public scare about comics and crime led publishers to adopt a strict censorship code. Its big rise came from the late 1980s, when specialist comic shops let hundreds of small publishers reach readers, and it is still near its peak.

Japan sits at the bottom, peaking in 2019. Manga boomed long before that, but the database holds few older Japanese series, so its strip leans late.

Countries did not only publish at different times. They published different kinds of comics.

Each country built its comics around different kinds of stories, and often the stories were someone else's.

Superheroes are the biggest genre in American comics, about a quarter of all stories. They make up even more in Brazil (63%) and Germany (44%), which print large numbers of American comics.

Norway, the Netherlands and Sweden built theirs on humour instead, and a lot of that humour stars a duck. Norway's weekly Donald Duck & Co has been published since 1948, and funny-animal stories are Norway's second-biggest genre after humour.

(Smaller genres are grouped together and not shown.)

Countries did not only publish different stories. They published them in different shapes.

Some countries launch thousands of short series. Others keep a few running for decades.

The United States publishes the most series, but they average only about 7 issues each. American publishers often restart a title at issue #1 to win new readers, and many stories come out as short limited series.

Mexico's series average about 121 issues. Its comics were cheap weeklies that ran for years under one title: El Libro Vaquero, a western launched in 1978, passed 1,500 issues and sold 1.5 million copies a week in the 1980s. Japan sits in between at about 37 issues per series, and Australia at about 10.

Japan is the clearest case of a country that built its own kind of comics, and then kept them for itself.

§2Japan makes its own comics, and keeps them.

Japan has the largest comics industry in the world, and almost all of it is homegrown. Modern manga took off after the Second World War, led by Osamu Tezuka, whose Astro Boy began in 1952. It has been sold ever since in thick weekly magazines: at its peak in 1995, Weekly Shōnen Jump sold about 6.5 million copies a week.

With that many stories of its own, Japan has never needed to import. Just 0.07% of the Japanese comic issues in the database are reprints of foreign work.

In 2025, Japanese readers spent ¥692.5 billion on manga, about US$4.4 billion. Almost all of it was written and drawn in Japan.

Digital manga grew three and a half times in ten years, from ¥149 billion in 2016 to ¥527 billion in 2025. Most of it is read on phones, in apps that sell a chapter at a time. It is now about three quarters of the market.

Print had one last surge. In 2020 and 2021, lockdowns and the Demon Slayer craze pushed printed volumes up to about ¥208 billion a year, before they fell to ¥126 billion in 2025.

(Print figures cover books and magazines sold through wholesalers. Digital figures are estimates of reader spending.)

Japan does not only read its own comics. It grows the people who draw them, all over the country.

Tokyo is the centre of Japanese publishing, but manga artists come from every corner of the country.

Per person, Tokyo is not the leader. Across all generations, rural Kōchi has produced about 38 manga artists for every million people, ahead of Tokyo's 34, with Akita and Iwate close behind. Tokyo tops only the map for artists born in the 1960s.

Kōchi takes manga seriously. Since 1992 it has hosted the Manga Kōshien, a national contest where high-school teams draw manga against the clock.

(Only artists with a recorded birthplace and birth year on Wikidata are counted, so famous artists are over-represented. Rates use today's population, not the population when each artist was born.)

What those artists draw, and who for, has changed too.

Manga is not one genre. It is sold by the reader it is written for.

Manga for adult men, seinen, became the biggest band in the 1990s and has kept pace with shōnen, the boys' adventure manga most Western readers know: 566 popular series started in the 2010s against 537. Shōjo, for teenage girls, shrank the most.

Seinen grew because manga's first readers grew up. Publishers launched magazines to keep them, like Weekly Young Jump in 1979 and Morning in 1982.

(Based on the 5,000 manga with the most members on MyAnimeList, an English-language site. It shows what international fans follow, not everything published in Japan.)

So who is manga written for right now?

Look only at the series that were still being published in 2023.

Manga for adult men, seinen, now slightly outnumbers manga for teenage boys, shōnen: 218 running series against 211. Almost half of all popular running manga are written for adults.

Some of these series have run for decades. One Piece, a shōnen adventure, began in 1997. Kingdom, a seinen war epic, began in 2006. Both were still running in 2023.

(Same 5,000 MyAnimeList titles as the chart above.)

Japan makes comics for every age group. America's comics industry grew up differently.

§3America reprints itself.

The United States is the world's other comics superpower, and like Japan it mostly reprints itself. Only about 1% of American comic issues in the database are reprints of foreign work, while 11% are reprints of older American comics sold again as collected editions.

The superheroes are why. Superman and Batman have been published for more than 80 years, so their old stories never stop selling.

American comics have been made all over the country, but not evenly.

New York stands out on both counts: 1,775 creators, about 89 for every million residents. American comic books began there. DC and Marvel were both founded in New York in the 1930s, and the first comics were drawn in Manhattan studios by young New Yorkers like Jack Kirby and Stan Lee.

California has the next biggest circle, with 873 creators, but it is pale, at about 22 per million. After the tiny District of Columbia (about 70), Massachusetts, at 42 per million, is the next darkest.

Those creators work in an industry that now sells far more than superheroes.

Superhero comic books are what most people picture when they think of American comics. They are not what Americans buy most.

Adult fiction more than doubled in a single year, from 15.2 million copies in 2020 to 32.5 million in 2021. Stuck at home in lockdown, people streamed anime, then went looking for the manga it came from. Sales peaked at 35.4 million in 2022.

In 2023, manga made up 21.8 million of the 44.7 million graphic novels sold, almost half.

(BookScan covers bookshops and online retailers, about 85% of the book market, but not comic shops, libraries or school book fairs.)

America's bookshops are now full of comics from Japan. So are Australia's.

§4Australia's comics are mostly someone else's.

Australia has always read comics. For most of the last eighty years, it has printed other countries' comics to do it. That was the cheap option: a reprinted story is already written and drawn, so a publisher only pays to print it.

Start with today. Australians are buying more comics than ever.

Sales of graphic novels in Australian shops nearly tripled in three years, from about 500,000 copies in 2020 to 1.38 million in 2023.

Over the same years, the database records just 14 new Australian comic series. The two panels count different things, copies bought and series made, but together they show the gap: demand nearly tripled while local supply stayed in single figures each year.

It's the reprint problem again. An Australian comic has to pay for itself in a market of 27 million people. An imported one has already paid for itself in a market more than ten times bigger.

(Sales figures exclude manga and superhero titles, so the true total is higher. The newest years of the database are the least complete, because volunteers tend to catalogue recent comics last.)

So if Australians are not reading Australian comics, whose are they reading?

Look at the comics Australia has published itself, and many of them turn out to be someone else's.

About 18 of every 100 Australian issues reprint a foreign comic, and fewer than 1 reprints an Australian one. In Japan, foreign reprints do not fill a single square: about 7 in every 10,000 issues.

The best-known example is The Phantom. The American hero has been printed in Australia by Frew Publications since 1948, for more than 75 years.

(These are only the reprints volunteers have linked to their originals, so the true shares are higher everywhere.)

Australia is not the only country that imports its comics, but it is near the top of the list.

Every country reprints comics. What differs is whose.

Australia ranks third for foreign reprints, behind only Norway and Canada. Canada's story is close to Australia's. It also banned American comics during the Second World War, and when the ban ended, American comics flowed back across the border.

The United States and Japan sit at the bottom: when they reprint, they reprint themselves. Eleven of every 100 American issues bring back an older American comic.

(Yugoslavia is shown as it existed from 1918 to 1991.)

It was not always this way. For a few years, Australia made its own comics because it had no choice.

For nineteen years, from 1940 to 1959, no new American comics could be imported. The ban suited local publishers, who had been complaining about cheap American magazines being dumped on the Australian market.

New series grew from 2 in 1939 to 29 in 1944, and reached 85 in 1955. When American comics returned in 1959, local output fell. A second wave, Australian editions of American comics, peaked at 109 new series in 1982. After that, output almost disappeared.

(The chart shows three-year averages, so the single-year counts above sit higher than the strip.)

Where did the replacement comics come from? Almost entirely from one country.

American comics did not only fill Australian shelves. They filled shelves around the world.

Western Europe took the most, led by France with about 71,000 stories. Australia took 22,645, more than any other country outside Europe except Brazil. Most of those are the second wave from the chart above: American stories printed in Australian editions.

(Counts are reprinted stories rather than issues, and only stories that volunteers have linked to their American original.)

The readers are still here. The next question is where they gather.

The appetite for manga and anime shows up in person, too.

SMASH! grew from 1,435 visitors in 2007 to 53,071 in 2026, about 37 times bigger. It has grown every year since it returned after the pandemic, the same years manga sales boomed in America's bookshops.

Those fans are not spread evenly across the country.

Which comics Australians look for depends on where they live.

In every state and territory, Australians search for manga more than twice as often as for comics. The Northern Territory leans furthest towards manga, at 76% of searches, and Tasmania the least, at 67%.

(Google Trends shows relative interest, not the number of searches.)

The readers are here. The question is who will make comics for them.

§5Can we grow them at home?

The fastest-growing comic readers in Australia are the youngest.

Sales of children's graphic novels in Australia grew 3.3 times in three years, to almost 1.1 million copies in 2023. Adult fiction grew 1.8 times.

Most of those children's books are imported too, led by American series like Dav Pilkey's Dog Man. A generation of Australian kids is growing up reading comics, mostly made somewhere else.

Australia has made comics before. In the 1940s, when imports were banned, a local industry grew within a few years. The readers are here now in greater numbers than ever. The open question is whether Australian comics can find them without a ban to clear the shelves.
