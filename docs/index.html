Australia reads comics. Australia doesn't make them.

Who makes the world's comics, and who only reads them, 1930 to 2026

A comic can be made in Australia, or made somewhere else and printed here. This page follows both: comics made in Australia, comics made in Japan and the United States, and the reprints that carry them around the world.

Unless a chart says otherwise, these figures count comic issues, not copies sold. They come from the Grand Comics Database, a volunteer catalogue of over two million comic issues from around the world.

By Thisum Patabadige · October 2026 · Sources listed at the end of the page

§1Comics are everywhere. They are not made everywhere.

Almost every country reads comics. Far fewer make them, and the ones that do have made them at different times, in different amounts, and about different things.

Start with where comics come from. Counted in total, a handful of large countries publish most of the world's comics.

Counted per person, the map changes. Small countries in northern Europe turn the darkest.

Norway has about 10,600 comic issues for every million people, around six times the rate of the United States. Australia sits at about 1,300 per million.

(The database is built by volunteers, so this map also measures how thoroughly each country's comics have been catalogued. Japan, the largest comics market in the world, shows only about 1,100 per million for that reason. Colours use a log scale, so each step is about ten times the last.)

So who makes comics depends on how you count. The next question is when.

Comic industries did not grow at the same time. Some countries had their boom before television arrived, others are still in theirs.

(Reading the ridgeline: each strip is one country's new comic series per year. The taller the strip, the closer that year was to the country's busiest. Countries run from the earliest publishers at the top to the latest at the bottom.)

Australia's strip has two humps: one in the 1950s, when local publishers filled the gap left by banned imports, and another around 1980, when Australian editions of American comics boomed. After the mid-1980s it almost disappears.

(Series with an uncertain start year are left out, so the earliest decades are undercounted.)

Countries did not only publish at different times. They published different kinds of comics.

Each country built its comics around different kinds of stories.

(Each row is one country and each column one genre. Darker purple means a larger share of that country's stories.)

Superheroes dominate American comics. Most other countries built their industries on humour, adventure and stories for children.

(A story can carry more than one genre, so shares count genre tags rather than whole stories. Smaller genres are grouped together and not shown.)

Countries did not only publish different stories. They published them in different shapes.

Some countries launch thousands of short series. Others keep a few running for decades.

(Each dot is a country. Further right means more series published. Higher up means each series ran for more issues on average.)

The United States publishes the most series, but they average only about 4 issues each. Mexico's average about 119. Japan sits in between at about 37 issues per series, and Australia at about 10.

(Both axes use a log scale, so each gridline multiplies the value rather than adding to it. Countries with fewer than 200 series in the database are left out.)

Japan is the clearest case of a country that built its own kind of comics, and then kept them for itself.

§2Japan makes its own comics, and keeps them.

Japan has the largest comics industry in the world, and almost all of it is homegrown. Just 0.07% of the Japanese comic issues in the database are reprints of foreign work. In 2025 the Japanese manga market was worth ¥692.5 billion, even as print sales fell and more readers moved to digital.

In 2025, Japanese readers spent ¥692.5 billion on manga, about US$4.4 billion. Almost all of it was written and drawn in Japan.

(Each band is one format. The top edge of the chart is the whole market.)

Digital manga grew three and a half times in ten years, from ¥149 billion in 2016 to ¥527 billion in 2025. It is now about three quarters of the market, while printed volumes and magazines have both shrunk.

(Print figures cover books and magazines sold through wholesalers. Digital figures are estimates of reader spending.)

Japan does not only read its own comics. It grows the people who draw them, all over the country.

Tokyo is the centre of Japanese publishing, but manga artists come from every corner of the country.

(Each map is one generation, grouped by the decade an artist was born. Darker purple means more manga artists for every million people living in that prefecture today.)

Per person, several rural prefectures have produced as many manga artists as Tokyo, in every generation.

(Only artists with a recorded birthplace and birth year on Wikidata are counted, so famous artists are over-represented. Rates use today's population, not the population when each artist was born.)

What those artists draw, and who for, has changed too.

Manga is not one genre. It is sold by the reader it is written for.

(Reading the streamgraph: each band is one audience, and its thickness shows how many popular series started that year. Greens are manga written for teenagers, teals manga written for adults.)

Manga written for adult men, seinen, has grown fastest among popular titles, and now rivals shōnen, the boys' adventure manga most Western readers know.

(Based on the 5,000 manga with the most members on MyAnimeList, an English-language site. It shows what international fans follow, not everything published in Japan.)

So who is manga written for right now?

Look only at the series that were still being published in 2023.

(Each slice is one audience. The wider and longer the slice, the more series.)

Manga for adult men, seinen, now slightly outnumbers manga for teenage boys, shōnen: 218 running series against 211. Almost half of all popular running manga are written for adults.

(Same 5,000 MyAnimeList titles as the chart above, counting the 492 still being published in August 2023 that have a stated audience. Another 520 running series list no audience and are left out.)

Japan makes comics for every age group. America's comics industry grew up differently.

§3America reprints itself.

The United States is the world's other comics superpower, and like Japan it mostly reprints itself. Only about 1% of American comic issues in the database are reprints of foreign work, while 11% are reprints of older American comics sold again as collected editions.

American comics have been made all over the country, but not evenly.

(Each circle is one state. A bigger circle means more comic creators were born there. A darker circle means more for every million people living there today.)

New York stands out on both counts: 1,775 creators, about 89 for every million residents. California has the next biggest circle, with 873 creators, but it is pale, at about 22 per million. After the tiny District of Columbia (about 70), Massachusetts, at 42 per million, is the next darkest.

(Counts only creators in the Grand Comics Database with a recorded birth state. Rates use 2024 Census Bureau population estimates.)

Those creators work in an industry that now sells far more than superheroes.

Superhero comic books are what most people picture when they think of American comics. They are not what Americans buy most.

(Each line is one kind of graphic novel sold in US bookshops and online. Red marks adult fiction, which is mostly manga.)

Adult fiction nearly doubled, from 15.2 million copies in 2020 to 28.2 million in 2023, after peaking at 35.4 million in 2022. In 2023, manga made up 21.8 million of the 44.7 million graphic novels sold, almost half.

(BookScan covers bookshops and online retailers, about 85 per cent of the book market, but not comic shops, libraries or school book fairs.)

America's bookshops are now full of comics from Japan. So are Australia's.

§4Australia's comics are mostly someone else's.

Australia has always read comics. For most of the last eighty years, it has printed other countries' comics to do it.

Start with today. Australians are buying more comics than ever.

Sales of graphic novels in Australian shops nearly tripled in four years, from about 500,000 copies in 2020 to 1.38 million in 2023.

Over the same four years, the database records just 14 new Australian comic series. The two panels count different things, copies bought and series made, but together they show the gap: demand nearly tripled while local supply stayed in single figures each year.

(Sales figures exclude manga and superhero titles, so the true total is higher. The newest years of the database are the least complete, because volunteers tend to catalogue recent comics last.)

So if Australians are not reading Australian comics, whose are they reading?

Look at the comics Australia has published itself, and many of them turn out to be someone else's.

(Reading the waffle: each grid is 100 comic issues. Dark squares reprint a foreign comic, light squares reprint one of the country's own, and grey squares have no reprint recorded.)

About 18 of every 100 Australian issues reprint a foreign comic, and fewer than 1 reprints an Australian one. In Japan, foreign reprints do not fill a single square: about 7 in every 10,000 issues.

(These are only the reprints volunteers have linked to their originals, so the true shares are higher everywhere.)

Australia is not the only country that imports its comics, but it is near the top of the list.

Every country reprints comics. What differs is whose.

(Bars to the right show reprints of foreign comics. Bars to the left show reprints of a country's own older comics.)

Australia ranks third for foreign reprints, behind only Norway and Canada. The United States and Japan sit at the bottom: when they reprint, they reprint themselves. Eleven of every 100 American issues bring back an older American comic.

(Countries with more than 5,000 issues in the database. Yugoslavia is shown as it existed from 1918 to 1991.)

It was not always this way. For a few years, Australia made its own comics because it had no choice.

In 1940, wartime import controls stopped American comics reaching Australian newsstands. Local publishers filled the gap almost overnight.

(Reading the horizon chart: the strip shows new Australian series each year. Light gold fills up to half the busiest year. When a year is busier than that, the extra folds back over the strip in dark gold, so dark gold always marks the busiest years.)

New series jumped from 2 in 1939 to 83 in 1940, and the industry peaked in the mid-1950s. When American comics returned in 1959, local output began to fall. After a second wave of Australian editions of American comics around 1980, it almost disappeared.

(Series with an uncertain start year are left out. Each point is a three-year average.)

Where did the replacement comics come from? Almost entirely from one country.

American comics did not only fill Australian shelves. They filled shelves around the world.

(Each line runs from the United States to a country that reprinted American stories. The thicker the line, the more stories.)

Western Europe took the most, led by France with about 71,000 stories. Australia took 22,645, more than any other country outside Europe except Brazil.

(Counts are reprinted stories rather than issues, and only stories that volunteers have linked to their American original. Flows of under 1,000 stories are not shown.)

The readers are still here. The next question is where they gather.

The appetite for manga and anime shows up in person, too.

(Each point is one year's attendance at SMASH!, Sydney's manga and anime convention. Hollow points are minimum figures.)

SMASH! grew from 1,435 visitors in 2007 to 53,071 in 2026, about 37 times bigger, and it has grown every year since it returned after the pandemic.

(Attendance as reported by the convention and collected on Wikipedia. Some years are reported only as 'more than' a figure, and none were published for 2016 and 2017.)

Those fans are not spread evenly across the country.

Which comics Australians look for depends on where they live.

(Each state is coloured by the share of Google searches for 'manga', compared with searches for 'comics'. The colour scale runs from 60 to 80 per cent, so even the palest state still searches mostly for manga.)

In every state and territory, Australians search for manga more than twice as often as for comics. The Northern Territory leans furthest towards manga, at 76 per cent of searches, and Tasmania the least, at 67 per cent.

(Google Trends shows relative interest, not the number of searches, over the past five years.)

The readers are here. The question is who will make comics for them.

§5Can we grow them at home?

The fastest-growing comic readers in Australia are the youngest.

(Grey dots are sales in 2020, gold dots sales in 2023. The longer the line, the bigger the growth.)

Sales of children's graphic novels in Australia grew 3.3 times in three years, to almost 1.1 million copies in 2023. Adult fiction grew 1.8 times.

(Excludes manga and superhero titles, which BookScan counts separately. Most of the best-selling children's titles are imported, such as Dog Man.)

Australia has made comics before. In the 1940s, when imports were banned, a local industry appeared almost overnight. The readers are here now in greater numbers than ever. The open question is whether Australian comics can find them without a ban to clear the shelves.
